---
title: Computational Dialectics
subtitle: Modeling competing legal positions as structured arguments and responses
---

<Callout intent="info">
Computational dialectics is intended to expose the structure of disagreement. It does not decide which party is correct merely because one position can be represented more neatly.
</Callout>

## Core objects

`POSITION` — a party, court, legislature, or analyst's stated position.

`CLAIM` — a proposition within that position.

`SUPPORT` — authority, evidence, or reasoning offered for a claim.

`ATTACK` — a contradiction, exception, evidentiary challenge, or competing reason.

`DEFENSE` — a response preserving or narrowing a challenged claim.

`CONCESSION` — an expressly accepted proposition or limitation.

`STATUS` — supported, challenged, defended, unresolved, withdrawn, or superseded.

## Example structure

`CLAIM A`

→ supported by `AUTHORITY 1`

→ attacked by `CLAIM B`

→ defended by `AUTHORITY 2`

→ remains `CONTESTED`

The eventual graph can connect each node to the exact source material from which it was derived.

## Why this matters for case analysis

A judicial opinion often contains multiple layers of reasoning: competing factual propositions, alternative legal tests, responses to arguments, and reasons for rejecting or limiting a position. Flattening those relationships into a summary can erase the actual dialectical structure.

The system should therefore preserve disagreement instead of prematurely resolving it.
